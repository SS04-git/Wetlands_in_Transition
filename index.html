This study follows how the estuary changes with the tide, the seasons and the years, and how that change is represented in formal land-use planning.

One question runs through the six maps that follow: where does the land end and the water begin?

Each dot carries the colour of the satellite image at that point. The frame, about 250 by 195 metres, is the view in which every date was traced.

Map 1 · The estuary as a landscape

One day, pulled apart.

What is here on a single day?

The satellite photo of 25 February 2025 comes first. Watch each kind of thing being traced over it, one at a time. Then every part is lifted into its own layer, like the pages of a book held apart.

Map 1 · What to look for

  • Water: on this day it covers almost the whole frame. No bare estuary bed was showing.
  • Land edge: one thin line where the land stops and the water or bed begins.
  • Boats and cage farms: each is a single dot that marks where it was, not how big it is.

Map 1 · Mangrove canopy

Mangroves in the middle of the water.

Six patches of mangrove sit on the bed at the centre of the estuary. Only their leafy tops were traced, the part you can see from above, not the roots or the whole habitat.

Map 1 · The shore

Boats along the shore, cages out on the water.

The boats sit in a line at the water's edge, just inside the land edge. Further out, the cage farms float in open water: cages where fish are raised.

The printed map shows these traces over a recent satellite photo, which was not taken on this date. The traces themselves are the evidence.

In shortEven a single day holds water, mangroves, boats and cage farms, with the land edge running through them.

Which places are always wet, and which change?

0 of 17 dates counted

The estuary is cut into small squares, each 1.5 m across. For each of the 17 dates, every square that was under water gets one ball. So the columns grow date by date.

water on 1 date now and then on all 17 dates

Height counts dates. It is not the depth of the water or the height of the ground.

Map 2 · Where do you draw the line?

Where would you draw the water's edge?

A map has to decide where the water ends. Move the slider to choose a rule: a square counts as water if it was wet on at least that many dates. Only the squares that pass stay lit.

Ask for just 1 date and the water looks huge. Ask for all 17 and it shrinks. Neither is wrong: the edge depends on the rule.

The printed map calls this algorithmic relaxation: turning a yes or no into a matter of degree. Worked out from the traced shapes for this site, so the figures may differ slightly from the printed map.

Map 2 · Mangrove canopy persistence

Mangroves that came back.

The same columns, now counting mangroves: one ball for every date a square showed mangrove canopy.

Mangroves were seen in 2011, then missing from all four images between 2013 and 2016, then seen on every date from December 2017 onwards. The tallest columns mark where they were seen most often.

Map 2 · Ground that comes and goes

Dry one day, under water the next.

Now the columns count bare ground: one ball for every date a square showed dry estuary bed.

Look at the band of middle-height columns. That is ground that was dry on some dates and under water on others, exactly what a single fixed line on a map cannot show.

The tide was not recorded, so some of this coming and going may simply be high and low tide.

In shortThe edge is not one line but a band that is sometimes wet and sometimes dry.

Next: the people who live and work along the edge.

Map 4 · The estuary as a lived landscape

Where people meet the water.

How do people live and work along the edge?

Two strips of the same shore, 2011 below and 2025 above. Houses, the track, trees, boats and fish cages are built up in 3D, so the shore reads as a place where people live and work. For the Kharvi community, this is where fishing, clam collecting and the sorting and cleaning of shellfish happen.

The map shows only what can be seen from above. It does not say who uses these places or how.

Map 4 · 2011 and 2025

Switch between the two years and look for what changed along the shore. Cage farms are cages floating in the water where fish are raised.

Buildings, boats and cage farms were marked as single points, so their shapes here are symbols, not measured outlines. The 2011 track looks narrower, but the two images are not equally sharp, so this is not counted as a change.

In shortThe changing edge is also where people work.

Does the town plan see the zone between land and water?

Two pictures of the same place. First, the ground as it was traced on 25 February 2025: water, mangroves, the land edge, boats and cage farms.

Map 5 · On the plan

Kundapura Master Plan 2031.

The town's official plan for how land is used. It gives every place one of four labels: developed, agriculture, marshy or water.

Existing land use, Drawing No. 2 (provisional, 1:15,000), shown here as dots. The plan says nothing about seasons or tides.

Map 5 · The study area on the plan

The same place, drawn twice.

The red box marks the study area on the plan, at the edge of the river. The dotted line joins it to the traces on the ground.

The plan was not pinned to real map coordinates, so it sits beside the traces rather than on top of them, and not at the same scale.

Map 5 · Laid over the ground

The plan, laid over the ground.

The box is lifted out of the plan and stretched over the traces. Inside it, the plan is almost all one colour, the blue it uses for the river. As it fades, the ground shows through: water, mangrove canopy, boats and cage farms.

Stretched to fit for comparison only. The two are not at the same scale.

Map 5 · Four labels

The plan in 100 squares.

The whole planning area, cut into 100 squares of 1% each and built as a small model: buildings for developed land, furrowed fields for agriculture, moving water for the water sheet.

Developed and agriculture take two thirds. Marshy Area, the plan's only in-between label, gets just 2 of the 100 squares. Here they shift between land and water, which no other square on the plan is allowed to do.

    Each square is 1% of the 1,741.74 ha planning area, rounded to whole squares.

    Plan and ground, side by side.

    Next: the monsoon that the images cannot see.

    Where plan and observation differ, it is treated as something to investigate, not as an error.

    Map 6 · The estuary and the monsoon

    The season the camera cannot see.

    What happens in the months the images cannot show?

    From June to September, rain and cloud hide the estuary from satellites, so the monsoon is missing from every image. Here it comes back as rain. Each of the 17 dates has two columns of balls:

    • Blue counts the rain of the monsoon before that date. One ball is 100 mm.
    • White counts the rain in the 30 days just before it. One ball is 5 mm.

    Map 6 · In focus

    • Blue monsoon rain · one ball = 100 mm
    • White the 30 days before · one ball = 5 mm
    • Wettest month before a date: 79.8 mm in the 30 days before 7 May 2022. Before five of the dates, no rain fell at all.
    • Monsoon totals: from 2,272 mm in 2018 to 3,848 mm in 2020.

    The rainfall comes from NASA POWER (MERRA-2), averaged over squares about 50 km wide, so it describes the region rather than the rain on the estuary itself.

    In shortEvery image follows a monsoon that the images themselves cannot show.

    Can a computer do the tracing?

    For every map so far, I traced 17 satellite images by hand, marking each patch as water, mangroves, mud and so on.

    Then I taught a computer to do the same. It looked at my tracing, learnt what each kind of ground looks like, and traced the images on its own. Below, this is called the model.

    2. Can the computer see it?
    3. What might happen next?

    What this is

    A study of the Panchagangavali estuary at Kundapur, Karnataka, where the river meets the Arabian Sea. It is an ecological and livelihood hub for the Kharvi community, who fish, collect clams and gather, sort and clean shellfish along its edges.

    The study follows how the edge between land and water changes with the tide, the seasons and the years, and how that change is drawn in formal land-use planning.

    Where does the land end and the water begin?

    How it was made

    1. 01Seventeen images

      Satellite images of the same frame were collected from Google Earth Pro. Rain and cloud hide the estuary from June to September, so every usable date falls between November and May.

    2. 02Traced by hand

      On each date the ground was traced into water, muddy shallows, bed ground, mangrove canopy, the land edge, tree cover, track, buildings, boats and cage farms.

    3. 03Six maps

      The tracings were compared across the dates, set beside the Kundapura Master Plan 2031 and the monsoon rainfall, and drawn as six A3 maps.

    4. 04A computer test

      A computer model was taught to trace the same images, to see how reliably the change can be read from them.
